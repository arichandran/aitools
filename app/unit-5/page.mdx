### <a id="_Toc239678009"></a>5\.1 Bias and Hallucination in AI Outputs

Hallucination refers to an AI model generating text that is fluent and confident-sounding but factually incorrect or entirely fabricated — for example, inventing a citation, a statistic, or a historical date that does not exist. This happens because, as explained in Unit 1 (Section 1.3), an LLM generates the statistically most plausible next token, not a verified fact; when it lacks reliable information on a topic, it can still produce a plausible-sounding but false continuation rather than admitting uncertainty.

Bias refers to systematic skew in a model's outputs that reflects imbalances or stereotypes present in its training data — for instance, associating certain professions more strongly with one gender, or giving less accurate answers about regions/cultures underrepresented in the training corpus. Both hallucination and bias are technical properties of how these models are built, not intentional deception, but they carry the same practical implication: every AI output requires human judgement before being trusted or acted upon.

### <a id="_Toc239678010"></a>5\.2 Academic Integrity and Citation of AI Assistance

Most universities, including JNTUK\-affiliated institutions, now require students to disclose the use of AI tools in coursework, similar to citing any other source\. Appropriate disclosure typically states: which tool was used, for what part of the work, and to what extent \(e\.g\., brainstorming only, vs\. drafting full paragraphs, vs\. grammar\-checking\)\. Using AI to generate ideas or explanations to learn from is generally acceptable; submitting AI\-generated content as entirely one's own original work, without disclosure, in place of demonstrating one's own understanding, constitutes academic dishonesty under most institutional policies\.

### <a id="_Toc239678012"></a>5\.4 Copyright and Intellectual Property in AI\-Generated Content

Two separate IP questions arise with generative AI: \(a\) Does the AI\-generated output infringe someone else's copyright? — this can occur if the model reproduces or very closely paraphrases a substantial portion of a specific copyrighted work \(a song, a textbook passage, a piece of art\) from its training data\. \(b\) Who owns the AI\-generated output? — this is a legally evolving area; many jurisdictions currently hold that purely AI\-generated content \(with no meaningful human creative input\) may not be eligible for copyright protection at all, while human\-edited/curated AI\-assisted work may qualify depending on the degree of human authorship\. Students should treat close paraphrasing of a specific, identifiable source \(be it AI\-generated or not\) with the same caution as direct copying\.

### <a id="_Toc239678013"></a>5\.5 Emerging Trends: Multimodal AI and AI Agents

Multimodal AI refers to models that can accept and/or generate more than one type of content — text, images, audio, and video together — within a single interaction \(e\.g\., uploading a photo of a hand\-drawn circuit diagram and asking the model to explain it, or asking it to generate an image and then discuss it\)\. AI agents extend a model beyond single\-turn question\-answering into autonomously carrying out multi\-step tasks: planning a sequence of actions, using external tools \(web search, code execution, file editing\), checking its own intermediate results, and continuing until the overall goal is achieved with minimal step\-by\-step human instruction \(e\.g\., Claude Code autonomously reading a codebase, writing a fix, running tests, and iterating\)\. For future engineering practice, this means AI is shifting from being a tool that answers one question at a time to being a collaborator capable of executing entire sub\-projects, which raises correspondingly larger questions of oversight, verification, and accountability for its output\.
